# Section 2.1 The recycling rate problem, revised

Word count: 1,615 to 1,278. Paragraphs: 12 to 10.

Since 2010 the quantity of e-waste generated worldwide has grown five times faster than compliant collection and recycling. In 2022 only 22.3% of the waste electrical and electronic equipment generated was documented as formally collected and recycled, and on a business-as-usual trajectory that rate is projected to fall to 20% by 2030 (Baldé et al., 2024).

In the past decade, European legislation has adopted measures to act against this decline rate. Since 15 August 2018, Directive 2012/19/EU has covered all electrical and electronic equipment. It sorts that equipment into six categories. Three are defined largely by what the equipment is: temperature exchange equipment, screens, and lamps. Three are defined by its size: large equipment above 50 cm, small equipment below it, and small information technology and telecommunication equipment. Since 2019, Member States must collect annually either 65% of the average weight of equipment placed on their market in the three preceding years, or 85% of the waste equipment generated on their territory, two bases the Directive itself treats as broadly equivalent (Directive 2012/19/EU, Art. 7 and recital 17).

Vehicles are outside that scope, and their electronics fall under a separate end-of-life regime whose targets are also set by weight (Directive 2000/53/EC, Art. 7(2)(b)). Mass is a poor proxy for what those components hold. The waste electrical and electronic equipment generated in 2022 contained 31 billion kg of metals alongside 17 billion kg of plastics and 14 billion kg of other materials (Baldé et al., 2024), and within the metal fraction value is concentrated rather than spread. Gold, silver and palladium constitute about 90% of the inherent value of high- and medium-grade discarded printed circuit boards (Oke & Potgieter, 2024). Individual components carry that concentration further still, in proportions set by their function. Tantalum capacitors are between 30% and 40% tantalum by weight, and approximately 34% of world tantalum production was used in tantalum capacitors in 2016 (Oke & Potgieter, 2024).

The electronic content of a vehicle is not uniform, and it is changing. Restrepo et al. (2019) applied statistical learning to 65,475 car models sold in Switzerland over fourteen years, in support of a proposed national extension of waste electrical and electronic equipment rules to devices in end-of-life vehicles, and their results separate two influences on what a vehicle carries. Regulation determines which systems become universal: those affected by safety, fuel efficiency or emissions rules, such as the electronic stability program and the stop-start system, rose rapidly to high penetration, while systems left to the market, such as navigation and cruise control, held at intermediate levels for years. Market position determines which cars carry the rest, since the four characteristics that best predict the presence of a given system are price, mass, engine displacement and production year.

The more consequential trend is integration. Functions that once occupied their own unit are absorbed into higher-level systems, and the physical form of a new control system varies: it may be a dedicated electronic control unit, an integrated circuit inside an existing unit, or a software addition (Restrepo et al., 2019). The anti-lock braking system shows the pattern. Its standalone penetration fell from almost 60% in 2001 to below 20% in 2015 as the electronic stability program, which contains the braking function, rose to around 80%, while the two together have remained close to 100% since 2005 (Restrepo et al., 2019, p.10).

Integration also separates mass from value. The number of physical control units is expected to fall and individual devices to become lighter through miniaturization, yet total critical raw material stocks across the fleet may still rise, because penetration increases in every car type. Restrepo et al. (2019) draw the practical conclusion themselves: a list of devices that must be dismantled would need constant revision, and would hold up better if defined by vehicle function than by named part, because a rule written around a function still catches whichever device performs it, while a rule naming a part stops working as soon as that part is absorbed into another. Their underlying diagnosis is blunter. The use of these systems in vehicles and their composition "is poorly understood, which hampers the development of recycling strategies for CRMs in cars" (Restrepo et al., 2019, p.2).

Chancerel et al. (2009) divide the recycling of waste electrical and electronic equipment into collection, preprocessing and end processing, where preprocessing covers pre-sorting, liberation through manual dismantling or shredding, and manual or mechanical separation. Preprocessing determines which fractions, and therefore which substances, are steered into the appropriate end-processing streams. A target substance sorted into the wrong output stream at this stage is in most cases lost at the final recovery step, so the effectiveness of preprocessing governs the recovery of that substance across the entire chain.

What preprocessing can achieve is constrained by decisions taken years before disposal, and those decisions are not simply oversights. Peitzmeier et al. (2025) argue that existing circular frameworks for the automotive industry lack specific detail on vehicle electronics at both product and component level, and respond with a Physical Component Mapping method to represent the interfaces of automotive electronics together with an Eco-Sensitivity Framework to guide design. Where the constraint is physical rather than procedural it is harder still to remove. Circuit boards and printed electronics are encapsulated in plastic to protect them from corrosion and mechanical damage, and that encapsulation is precisely what makes them difficult to recycle at end of life (Harkema et al., 2024). The two requirements are in direct conflict, since an encapsulant that can be separated is one that adheres less strongly than structural integrity demands.

The literature does not, however, endorse manual disassembly as the remedy. Oke & Potgieter (2024) report that manual dismantling of electronic components shows low disassembly efficiency and exposes operators to hazards including electrolytic capacitor explosions and the release of poisonous gases, concluding that manual dismantling "fails to meet the prerequisites of safe production and presents difficulties in achieving large-scale application" (Oke & Potgieter, 2024, p.6). Alcoceba-Pascual et al. (2025) reach a related conclusion from a different direction. Dismantling two electronic parts from a vehicle raised recovery, measured as Mineral Capital, from 0.03% and 5% under shredding to 59% and 49% respectively, but disassembling those parts further improved the result by only two percentage points for one part and slightly worsened it for the other. A third position holds that the selection should be automated rather than performed by hand. Maurice et al. (2021) report work in which tantalum capacitors are located on boards by a machine vision system and a trained convolutional neural network, then removed mechanically by a scraper mounted on a robotic arm, at an initial investment of EUR 60,000.

The objection is narrower than it first appears. What these studies reject is exhaustive manual disassembly, not the selective removal of identified components. Charles et al. (2020) note that isolating every surface-mounted device by hand costs more in time and money than the recovered material returns, but that the approach "may be suitable for 'cherry picking' of individual components identified as containing critical raw materials (CRMs) not targeted in precious metals (PM) recovery" (Charles et al., 2020, p.16), naming tantalum capacitors as the example. The automotive case points the same way, since tantalum is lost in every scenario examined unless the parts containing it are removed and processed separately (Alcoceba-Pascual et al., 2025). Therefore, selective removal depends on knowing which component holds the value, where it sits within the product, and why it repays the effort of removal. None of these studies addresses how the person performing the work acquires that knowledge.
