### 2.6 Synthesis

An electronic control unit is worth recovering for metals that make up almost none of its weight, and those
metals sit on one board inside it. Two consequences follow. A recovery target counted in mass does not
reward removing the part, because taking it out barely moves the number a dismantler reports. And a
dismantler who did want to remove it would still need to know which unit is worth opening and what is
inside it. The obstacle is not the difficulty of taking the part out. It is knowing that the part is worth
taking out.

European law is now producing exactly that knowledge. Producers will owe a description of the components
inside a vehicle, and a passport will carry it. A duty to publish is not the same as a duty to make usable,
and Adisorn et al. (2021) set the condition that decides the difference: whoever needs the information must
have the equipment to reach it, and must reach it with the least possible effort. Where passports have
already met physical recovery work, that condition is only half met. The record has been read by a sorting
machine, or by an operator at a computer beside the bench. In both cases the system identifies the object
and the information is then consulted away from it. The information reaches the plant. It does not reach
the hands.

Augmented reality does reach the hands, and the reviewed evidence says what it is good for. Its measured
benefit falls to the worker who knows least, and it helps more with finding and identifying a part than
with working faster. Every system reviewed here, however, produces its own instruction content, from
cameras, from design models, from scans of the object, or from a knowledge base its owner assembled. None
of them is fed by a standardised record issued with the product. So both halves of the problem now exist,
and they have not been joined.

Joining them cannot be assumed to help. The same literature reports that overlays invite over-reliance,
that their benefit is uneven between workers, and that showing too much hides the object the worker is
holding. The carrier these records are built on is not yet proven for arbitrary uses, network connections
in recovery facilities are unreliable, and nothing yet models whether an operator would accept the device
at all. A claim about the join therefore has to be measured rather than argued. Chapter 3 sets out how the
record was structured, how the part was assessed, how the consequences of its recovery routes were
modelled, and how the join was put in front of an operator.
