## 4.3 Test Participants Results

### 4.3.1 Participants

Nine participants took part in the test and are identified as P01 to P09. Two were in the 18 to 24
age group, four in the 25 to 34 group and three in the 45 and over group. Each reported a different
occupational field: electrician, electrical engineer, economist, marketing analyst, marketing and
social media, strategic design, innovation manager, student and retired.

Prior experience was self-rated on a five-point scale where 1 is never used and 5 is expert. For
head-mounted displays, four participants rated themselves at 1, two at 2, one at 3, one at 4 and one
at 5. For disassembling electronic devices, five rated themselves at 1, one at 2, none at 3, two at 4
and one at 5.

### 4.3.2 Task completion time and errors

Seventeen runs were recorded, nine with the Augmented Reality application and eight with the
two-dimensional manual. Every run reached the fifth and final step. In both conditions the completion
time is the sum of the five per-step values, so the two conditions are measured in the same way. In
five of the nine Augmented Reality runs the elapsed value written by the application differs from the
sum of its own per-step values, by at most 2 s; the sum of the steps is used throughout.

`[TABLE: Task completion time per participant, both conditions | source: own elaboration]`

`[FIGURE: Task completion time per participant, both conditions | source: own elaboration]`

The Augmented Reality condition took longer for five of the eight participants who performed both,
and less time for three. The individual differences run from 26 s faster to 25 s slower, and in
absolute terms from 15 s to 26 s. Across those eight participants the two-dimensional manual averages
312.5 s with a median of 300.0 s, and the Augmented Reality condition averages 318.2 s with a median
of 320.0 s. Taken over all nine Augmented Reality runs the mean is 329.3 s and the median 324.0 s.

The two conditions differ from one another by less than the participants differ from one another. The
manual times range from 201 s to 440 s and the Augmented Reality times from 186 s to 460 s, a spread
of 239 s and 274 s, while no participant's two times differ by more than 26 s. The participant with
the longest time in one condition also has the longest in the other, and the participant with the
shortest time in one has the shortest in the other. Across the eight participants the two conditions
correlate at r = 0.992.

Five of the eight participants who performed both conditions recorded a shorter time in the second
condition they performed, whichever condition that was. Of the four who began with the Augmented
Reality condition, the Augmented Reality run was the slower of their two runs for three. Of the four
who began with the two-dimensional manual, the Augmented Reality run was the slower of their two runs
for two.

`[TABLE or APPENDIX: per-step completion time, both conditions | source: own elaboration]`

The five steps take the same rank order in both conditions. The first two steps together account for
60.4 % of the mean total time in the manual condition and 60.7 % in the Augmented Reality condition,
and the fifth step is the shortest in both.

No error was recorded in any of the seventeen runs, in either condition. The unit used in the test is
an additively manufactured replica in which every part is a moulded polymer piece and no functional
electronic component is present.

### 4.3.3 Perceived usability and interview responses

Perceived usability was recorded with a ten-item questionnaire answered immediately after each
condition, every item on a five-point scale where 1 is disagree and 5 is agree. The ten responses
were converted into a single score between 0 and 100 for each participant and each condition. All
nine participants completed the questionnaire for both conditions, including P01, who answered the
manual items without performing a timed manual run.

`[FIGURE: Perceived usability score per participant, both conditions | source: own elaboration]`

`[TABLE: Perceived usability score per participant, both conditions | source: own elaboration]`

The score is higher for the Augmented Reality application than for the two-dimensional manual for
seven of the nine participants. The manual scores average 79.4 with a median of 85.0, and the
Augmented Reality scores average 83.9 with a median of 87.5. The individual differences run from 55.0
points below the manual to 32.5 points above it. The mean difference is 4.4 points and the median
difference is 12.5 points. The two differ in size: the single negative difference of 55.0 points
enters the mean and not the median.

P02 holds that difference, scoring the two-dimensional manual at 97.5 and the Augmented Reality
application at 42.5. P02 rated prior experience of disassembling electronic devices at 4 of 5 and
prior experience with head-mounted displays at 1 of 5.

After both conditions were finished, participants answered ten further items comparing the two
versions directly, each on a five-point scale where 1 is the two-dimensional manual and 5 is the
three-dimensional Augmented Reality model.

`[FIGURE: The ten comparative items, every response | source: own elaboration]`

The figure draws all ninety responses, one cell for each participant on each item. Every one of the
ten items returns a median of 5. Across the ten items and nine participants, 78 of the 90 responses
are 5, four are 3, two are 4 and six are 1, and five of the nine participants answered 5 on all ten
items. Two items were answered identically by every participant: which version taught more about what
the components are made of and their value, and which version was more engaging to use. Counted by
band, between seven and nine participants answered toward the Augmented Reality model on each item,
at most one answered at the midpoint of the scale and at most two answered toward the manual. P02 is
the only participant to answer below 3 on any item, and did so on five of the ten.

The first comparative item asks which version allowed the participant to work with more agility, and
the timed runs measure the same quantity independently. Of the eight participants who performed both
conditions, five took longer with the Augmented Reality application. Three of those five answered the
agility item toward the Augmented Reality model and two answered it toward the two-dimensional
manual.

The responses to the six open questions are the experimenter's written record of the closing
interview and are reported here as summary notes rather than as participant statements.

Four participants named the identification of tasks and components through the animations as the most
helpful feature. P01 named the design of the interface. P03 recorded that the application was most
valuable as a way to learn the sequence before handling the real component. P04, who reported no
background in electronics, recorded that the application made the components and their function
understandable. P09 named the component-level detail. P02 recorded that the value of the application
lies in devices where a mistake can injure the operator, and that on a prototype of this simplicity
it was disproportionate to the task.

Six participants named the sharpness of the passthrough image as the main difficulty, recording that
it reduced accuracy when unscrewing the fasteners on the physical unit. Three named the hand gestures
as the initial obstacle. P03 also recorded eye strain that began early in the session. P01 recorded
no difficulty.

On the gestures themselves, three participants recorded them as immediately intuitive and three
recorded that they became natural only after a demonstration and a few attempts. P08 recorded
difficulty at the first attempt. P02 recorded that the interaction became easier once it was clear
that the interface responds to a pinch rather than to a touch, and asked for an audible response on
button presses and on incorrect actions.

Eight of the nine recorded that the material and recovery information did not help them perform the
task on a prototype of this simplicity, and seven of those recorded that it nonetheless gave them
knowledge beyond conventional product specifications. P04 recorded the same in terms of transparency
about what the components are. P02 recorded that the information was closer to curiosity than to
assistance. P01 recorded that the information did influence how the task was performed.

All nine recorded that they would use the tool at a real recycling workstation. Eight named training
as the use and P02 named complex or dangerous devices instead. Seven named the comfort of the headset
over a long session as the constraint, and P02 recorded a limit of about fifteen minutes, locating it
in the hardware rather than in the application. No participant added anything further at the close of
the session.

## Six sentences that carry a risk, and what each rests on

**"in absolute terms from 15 s to 26 s"** — the eight differences are 20, 25, 19, 15, −15, −26, 23
and −15. Derived. The sentence is there so a reader cannot read the signed range as the whole story.

**"a spread of 239 s and 274 s, while no participant's two times differ by more than 26 s"** — this
is the strongest sentence in the block and it is arithmetic only. It says the instrument moved the
result less than the person did, without saying what follows from that.

**"Five of the eight recorded a shorter time in the second condition they performed"** — P02, P03,
P07, P08 and P09. This sentence exists to pre-empt the first question a supervisor asks about a
within-subject design. It states the count and stops.

**"three … of the four who began with the Augmented Reality condition"** — the same split, set
against the result. 🔴 **The reading that a practice effect contributes to the Augmented Reality
condition appearing slower is interpretation, and it belongs in the Discussion.** With four
participants on each side it is a direction, not a measurement, and the Discussion should say so.

**"60.4 % … and 60.7 %"** — the first two steps as a share of the mean total, per condition. Derived
from the per-step sheet.

**"No error was recorded"** — the property of the replica is stated beside it, with no causal word
joining the two. The explanation is the Discussion's.

## What is not in this section, deliberately

**Nothing from the session-report fields beyond the step count.** The application writes further
fields into every session report; none of them are reported here, and the only one used is that all
nine runs completed five steps.

**The instrument is not named.** The section reports a ten-item questionnaire, a five-point scale and
a score from 0 to 100, and makes no benchmark comparison. No citation is owed.

**No aggregate chart of the comparative items.** The figure draws every one of the ninety responses
as a cell and averages nothing, so it carries what the table carries. A bar chart of means or of
per-item distributions is not used: with 78 of 90 responses at the same value it would draw a
distribution that is not in the data.

**No quotation marks anywhere in the interview paragraphs.** The wording is yours throughout.

## Three things this section hands to the Discussion

1. **The simplicity of the replica**, which stands behind both the absent errors and the eight of
   nine who found the recovery information unhelpful for the task. One cause, two findings.
2. **Training rather than throughput.** Eight of nine name training, all nine would use it, and the
   times show no consistent gain. Three measures pointing the same way.
3. **The ceiling on the comparative items and the hardware ceiling.** Ten items returning the same
   median from the same five participants is a limit of the instrument on this sample; passthrough
   sharpness at six of nine and comfort at seven of nine are a limit of the device. Neither is a
   limit of the design.
