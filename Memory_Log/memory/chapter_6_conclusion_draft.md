This thesis asked how an Augmented Reality-based Digital Product Passport can assist a recycler in
disassembling a Vehicle Control Unit and in assessing the environmental consequences of its
end-of-life scenarios. The answer has two halves and they are not equally strong. A passport can
carry component-level material and impact data in a structure that any producer could populate, and
an Augmented Reality interface can put that record on the object itself, so the operator reads the
device and its data in one field of view. That much was built and it ran. What the work does not
show is that reading it there makes the work faster or recovers more material, and no claim of that
kind is made.

The first sub-question asked which component-level and environmental information is most relevant to
end-of-life stakeholders handling this device. The answer is the material composition and mass of
each component, the location of hazardous substances and substances of concern inside the housing,
the disassembly sequence with the tool each step needs, and the impact each recovery scenario
avoids. Two of these are spatial, and they are the two mandatory attributes the record could satisfy
only in part, because a document can state that a substance is present but not where it sits. The
limit encountered was never the structure. It was the values, since a party who is not the producer
cannot verify what the components are made of.

The second sub-question asked how that information can be delivered through an interface that
supports physical interaction with the product. It is delivered as three panels anchored in the room
beside the unit, with the record split into four views that must all be visited before the guided
sequence opens, an interactive model that can be handled and exploded, and a five-step flow that ends
in a stored summary. The disassembly instructions are discharged as an interaction rather than as a
document, which is the clearest single demonstration that the delivery channel changes what the
record can express.

The third sub-question asked how the Augmented Reality passport compares with a conventional
two-dimensional manual on task completion time, errors, and perceived usability. On time there is no
advantage. Five of the eight participants who performed both conditions were slower with the headset,
and the differences between participants are an order larger than the difference between conditions.
On errors the comparison is empty, because no error occurred in either condition on an object this
simple. On perceived usability the application is ahead, scoring higher for seven of the nine
participants, with every comparative item returning a median in favor of the model. The three
measures do not agree, and the disagreement is the finding.

The fourth sub-question asked what environmental difference results from guided dismantling compared
with current bulk practice. Under EF 3.1 characterization, guided dismantling avoids 3.22 times as
much resource use, minerals and metals as bulk shredding, and 1.85 times as much climate impact.
Adding component reuse raises those multiples to 4.74 and 3.49. The gross burdens of the four
scenarios cannot be told apart, so the difference exists entirely in what recovery displaces. The
ordering of the scenarios is robust and the absolute values are not.

The strengths and the weaknesses of the methodological approach both appeared in the findings rather
than remaining theoretical. Tagging every foreground quantity with its origin and its declared range
made the model's own resolution visible, and that visibility is what showed the gross comparison
between scenarios to be smaller than the uncertainty around it. Keeping burdens and credits in
separate product systems, never combined into one number, is what allowed the credit to be weighed on
its own when the burden turned out to carry no information. Recomputing every published table from
the raw data by a second route caught errors that a single calculation would have carried into the
document.

The weaknesses were equally visible. The reuse scenario rests on a yield that is quantified nowhere
in the literature, and it produced the widest uncertainty band in the study, so the most promising
result is also the least defensible one. The teardown model was designed to come apart, and that
single property explains both the absence of errors and the eight participants of nine who found the
material and recovery information unhelpful at the bench. The comparative questionnaire returned the
same median on all ten items from the same nine people, which is an instrument working at its
ceiling. Two results were not anticipated. The deterministic climate result sits well below the
median of its own simulated distribution, and the ranking of impact categories inverts between two
characterization methods for reasons that lie in their normalization references rather than in the
device.

For industry the finding that matters is that the structure is already satisfiable and the values are
not. A producer can populate this record today; nobody else can. Regulation (EU) 2026/1738 makes
information on the removal and replacement of parts obligatory from September 2029 and a Digital
Circularity Vehicle Passport from September 2032, and this work suggests two things about how that
record should be built. It has to carry location and not only presence, because the attributes an
operator needs most are spatial. And it has to declare the basis of every value, because a record
that mixes manufacturer data, assumptions and modelled output without saying which is which cannot be
audited.

For recyclers the finding is less comfortable. The recovery targets in the same Regulation are
counted in mass from January 2030, and the category dominating this device is created by a very small
mass of material. An operator who meets a mass target efficiently can still destroy most of the
environmental value in the unit. The information duty and the mass target point in different
directions, and only the first of them rewards removing a component of this size.

For the scientific community the contribution is narrow and specific. The literature had delivered
passport data to a sorting machine and to an operator at a computer beside the bench, and had
delivered augmented reality guidance authored inside a single organization. Nothing had tested a
standardized record, issued with the product, read in the operator's field of view. This work built
that combination and ran it with nine people, and the result is a feasibility demonstration rather
than an efficacy claim.

Four directions follow from what was not tested. The first is the operator. Every participant here
met the device for the first time, and the one participant with real disassembly experience was the
one who rejected the application as disproportionate to the task, which suggests the value of the
tool is a function of how much the operator already knows. A study with professional dismantlers on a
device they have never opened would test that directly. The second is the object. The teardown model
had no glued joints, no conformal coating and no electronic components, and a real unit has all
three, so the guidance was never tested against the physical resistance that decides whether reuse is
possible at all. The third is the record itself. Every material value used here is an assumption, and
a passport populated by the producer with verified values would answer a question this work could
only pose. The fourth is architecture. The record was served from a file on one machine, and nothing
in this work addresses how such a record is stored, versioned, secured or shared between the parties
that need it.

Two further directions extend the interface rather than the study. Guidance delivered in the field of
view is one channel, and a dismantling station is likely to become a shared one. Chen et al. (2023)
report augmented reality supporting human and robot working together on waste sorting, and a
dismantling cell in which a machine performs the repetitive separations while the operator handles
the judgements would need exactly the kind of shared, component-level record this thesis specifies.
The second direction is a layer this work deliberately excluded. The prototype delivers a fixed
sequence read from a static record, and an assistant able to interpret a device it has not seen
before, or to adapt a sequence when a component is damaged or missing, would remove the constraint
that every passport must be authored in advance. This is the author's expectation rather than a
finding, and nothing in this study tests it.

# 6.1 ANSWERING THE RESEARCH QUESTIONS — FINAL, agreed 2026-08-26

Thiago's own version, grammar corrected, with citations restored (the SRH template requires them in
every chapter) and two substantive corrections to claims that contradicted his own results.

This thesis set out to answer how an Augmented Reality-based Digital Product Passport can help a
recycler disassemble a Vehicle Control Unit and understand what its end-of-life scenarios cost the
environment. The answer can be divided into two parts, and they do not point the same way. On the
positive side, this study built an application in Augmented Reality that displays the Digital Product
Passport of the unit and lets the user work on the physical unit while interacting with digital
panels that provide the information needed to dismantle it. What the prototype could not achieve is
an advantage in time over a two-dimensional manual, or physical evidence that using it leads to the
kind of selective dismantling and component reuse that Scenario 4 models in the life cycle
assessment.

The first sub-question asked which component-level and environmental information matters most to the
people who handle this device at the end of its life. The answer comes from the literature review
together with the life cycle model built in openLCA on the ecoinvent database. At component level,
the more transparency the passport can provide, the more useful it becomes. Showing the material
composition of each component, where it sits inside the housing and how to reach it addresses the
obstacle the literature names, since Restrepo et al. (2019, p. 2) report that the composition of
vehicle electronics is poorly understood and that this hampers the development of recycling
strategies for critical raw materials in cars. Knowing what a component is made of and where it sits
is what makes selective removal possible, because removing a part rather than shredding it avoids the
extraction of its material, and removing it intact enough to be reused avoids its manufacture as
well. The difficulty in building the passport here was not its structure but the values inside it,
because only the manufacturer of the reference product holds the data needed to populate a
component-level record and a faithful set of disassembly steps.

The second sub-question asked how that information can be delivered through an interface that lets
the user handle the product while reading it. After several versions, the arrangement that works best
is three panels, where the user has access to the Digital Product Passport while at the same time
interacting with a digital model of the unit to be disassembled.

The third sub-question asked how the Augmented Reality passport compares with an ordinary
two-dimensional manual on completion time, errors and perceived usability. The test with nine
volunteers answered this question, and the times show no gain, since five of the eight participants
who performed both conditions were slower with the headset, and the differences between one
participant and another are far larger than the difference between the two conditions. On errors
there is nothing to compare, because nobody made one in either condition on an object as simple as
this. On perceived usability the application is clearly ahead, with a higher score for seven of the
nine participants and every comparative item leaning toward the model. The three measures disagree
with one another, and that disagreement is itself the result.

The fourth sub-question asked what environmental difference guided dismantling makes against current
bulk practice. The life cycle model built in openLCA under EF 3.1 characterization answered this
question. Guided dismantling, Scenario 3, avoids 3.22 times as much resource use, minerals and metals
as bulk shredding does, and 1.85 times as much climate impact, while adding component reuse, Scenario
4, raises those multiples to 4.74 and 3.49. This result holds only inside the model, because the
prototype built and tested here is neither the reference unit nor a replica of it. Even so, the
uncertainty analysis and the cross-check against a second characterization method both confirm the
trend: the more selectively the unit is dismantled, the more primary production its recovery
displaces.

Both the strengths and the weaknesses of the approach showed up in the results rather than staying on
paper. Declaring the origin and the range of every modelled quantity is what made it possible to see
that the difference between the gross scenarios is smaller than the uncertainty surrounding it, and
choosing a teardown model simple enough to be opened again and again is what removed the very
conditions in which the record would have been needed.

# 6.2 OUTLOOK — draft, 2026-08-26

Same voice as 6.1: plain words, connected sentences. Citations carried, per the template ruling.

The prototype was built against a record that nobody is required to produce yet, and that is about to
change. Regulation (EU) 2026/1738 requires information on the removal and replacement of parts and
components from September 2029, sets its recovery targets from January 2030, and requires a Digital
Circularity Vehicle Passport from September 2032. From that point onward the values this study had to
assume become values that a producer has to supply, so the structure shown here stays the same while
the record inside it becomes trustworthy. What today shows assumed material data to a volunteer would
then be showing verified material data to someone with a legal reason to act on it, and that is the
condition under which a tool of this kind stops being a demonstration and starts being useful.

Two things follow for whoever designs that record, and both are recommendations rather than findings.
It has to carry location and not only presence, because the information an operator needs most is
about where a substance or a component sits inside the housing, and a written list cannot express
that. And every value has to say where it came from, because a record that mixes manufacturer data,
assumptions and modelled results without distinguishing between them cannot be checked by the person
relying on it.

There is also a tension inside the same regulation that this work could not resolve. A recovery
target counted in mass gives an operator no reason to remove a component whose environmental weight
comes from a very small amount of material, so somebody can meet that target efficiently and still
destroy most of the value in a control unit. The information duty and the mass target pull in
opposite directions, and only the information duty makes the kind of work described here worth doing.

The next step for the interface itself is an artificial intelligence layer sitting between the record
and the operator. The prototype built here follows a fixed sequence that somebody wrote in advance,
which means every device needs its own authored passport before the application can guide anyone
through it. An agent trained on disassembly practice and able to read a Digital Product Passport
directly would remove that limit, because it could work out a sequence for a device it has never
seen, adapt that sequence when a fastener is seized or a component is missing, and explain to the
operator why one part should come out before another. This is what the author expects rather than
something this study tested, and nothing in the results either supports or contradicts it.

The physical work is the part that should move away from the human hand, and here the literature is
already ahead of this thesis. Oke and Potgieter (2024, p. 6) report that manual dismantling of
electronic components shows low disassembly efficiency and exposes operators to hazards including
electrolytic capacitor explosions and the release of poisonous gases, concluding that it fails to
meet the prerequisites of safe production. Maurice et al. (2021) describe a system in which a machine
vision system and a trained neural network locate tantalum capacitors on a board and a scraper on a
robotic arm removes them, which is selective removal performed without a person touching the device
at all. Chen et al. (2023) show augmented reality supporting a person and a robot working together on
waste sorting. Put together, these point at a dismantling station where the robot performs the cuts,
the unscrewing and the separations that are repetitive or unsafe, the artificial intelligence layer
decides the sequence from the passport, and the person supervises rather than performs. The operator
would then check the work, handle the cases the machine cannot read, and take the decisions that
carry consequences, which is a better use of a trained human than opening a housing by hand.

What a station of that kind would need is also what this study could not test. Every participant met
the device for the first time, and the one person with real disassembly experience was the one who
felt the application was more than the task needed, which suggests that the value of the tool depends
on how little the operator already knows. The teardown model had no glued joints, no protective
coating and no electronic parts inside it, so the guidance was never tested against the physical
resistance that decides whether a component can be reused at all. Every material value in the model
was assumed rather than measured. And the record itself sat in a file on a single machine, while
nothing here addresses how such a record would be stored, kept in versions, protected, or shared
between the parties who need it. Those four gaps are the work that follows this one.
